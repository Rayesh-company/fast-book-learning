# Market alternatives: how B2C learners "learn a book" today

- **Date**: 2026-10-02
- **Ticket**: aubed9/chat-with-books#39 (parent map: #36)
- **Question**: How do B2C learners actually learn a book today — status quo, summary apps, AI chat-over-book tools, courses and communities — including the Farsi-speaking market? Plus a DRAFT differentiation hypothesis for the rebranded product (a consumer learns one book with a guided agent over the book's own text, page-level citations, Farsi-first prototype deployed).
- **Method**: live web research; every market claim carries a numbered source from the Sources section. Facts are marked **[F]**, hypotheses **[H]**. Prices are as displayed on 2026-10-02 and change often; third-party-reported prices are labeled as such. Where something could not be sourced (especially Farsi-market claims), that is stated openly rather than guessed.

## 1. Status quo: self-reading, notes, rereading

What the default behavior actually is and what it delivers:

- **[F]** Rereading, highlighting, and summarization are among the most popular study strategies students use, and all three were rated **low utility** in the Dunlosky et al. (2013) review of ten learning techniques; the only two rated **high utility** were **practice testing (retrieval practice)** and **distributed practice (spacing)**. Elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, and interleaved practice were rated moderate. [10]
- **[F]** Karpicke & Roediger (2008, *Science*): repeated retrieval practice produced large gains in long-term retention a week later, while repeated rereading (restudying) added little once material was once recallable — and students predicted the opposite, underestimating testing and overestimating rereading. [11]
- **[F]** Book non-completion is a recognized consumer pattern, though hard numbers are scarce. The best-known public proxy is the "Hawking Index" (Jordan Ellenberg, WSJ 2014) — an explicitly "not remotely scientific" measure built from Kindle popular highlights, which scored e.g. *Thinking, Fast and Slow* at 6.1% and *A Brief History of Time* at 6.6% "read before giving up." [12] It is a journalist's proxy, not market research — but it captured something real enough to enter common parlance. [12]
- **[H]** Reading a book once, straight through, delivers exposure and in-the-moment comprehension; without retrieval, elaboration, or spacing, durable retention of the book's actual argument is weak. This is an extrapolation from the laboratory evidence above [10][11] to book-length reading, not a directly measured fact about book readers.

**What "learning" the status quo delivers**: contact with the full text, at the reader's own pacing, with zero verification that anything was retained.

**Category observations**

- **[F]** The anchor brand of the category was absorbed into enterprise learning: Go1 (corporate learning content platform) acquired Blinkist in May 2023, citing ~30 million learners at acquisition, and now bundles consumer-style microlearning into enterprise offerings [15]. Blinkist's own current pricing page still sells direct to consumers [1].
- **[F]** Shortform is the only summary app found here that explicitly bundles exercises and community discussions into the product [3]; none of them claim page-level citations back to the book's own text, and none position themselves as "learn this one book properly."
- **[H]** Summary apps optimize for the feeling of informedness in minimal time, which is a real job — but it is a *different job* from learning a specific book's actual content. The Hawking-Index-style completion problem [12] suggests many buyers of full books want exactly that lighter job; the summary market may be the mature answer to it, leaving the "actually learn this book" job underserved. Hypothesis, not established.

## 3. AI chat-over-book tools

This is the closest adjacent category to the rebranded product.

- **Google NotebookLM (now "Gemini Notebook")** — **[F]** Google's official Workspace page: it "uses your uploaded sources to answer questions and fulfill requests, providing citations for verification"; accepts Docs, Slides, PDFs, text/markdown, web URLs, pasted text, YouTube URLs, audio; up to 500,000 words or 200 MB per source; generates Audio Overviews and Mind Maps; "included in all Google Workspace plans"; and "does not currently keep a history of questions and responses in chat." [8] Free tier exists; paid tiers (Plus/Pro) exist on the official plans page [9] — third-party breakdowns report free limits of 100 notebooks / 50 sources per notebook and paid plans from ~$5 to ~$200/mo [9] (third-party, verify before quoting). It is a general research/brainstorming assistant across *many* sources, not a guided one-book learning journey, and it is English-first in practice.
- **Amazon Kindle "Ask This Book"** — **[F]** AI Q&A about the book you're reading inside Kindle, spoiler-aware (answers only from pages already read); launched US, iOS Kindle app, English-language books; authors/publishers were not consulted and cannot opt out, sparking an author-rights backlash (Writer Beware: it "turns books into searchable, interactive products... a new format"). [14] Alongside it, Amazon shipped AI **Kindle Recaps** (April 2025) for series refreshers [13] and a spoiler-safe "Story So Far" catch-up [14]. It is Q&A bolted onto a reader inside a closed retail ecosystem — no learning journey, no user-visible evidence ledger, English-only today.
- **Basmo "ChatBook"** — **[F]** Basmo's official ChatBook page markets "chat with your books": ask any question, simulate a conversation with the author, instant summaries, translation; **the page does not specify whether answers quote the actual book text or how the AI sources its answers** [7] — i.e., citation mechanics are not a claimed feature.
- **Study Companion (studycompanion.io)** — **[F]** an AI study app explicitly marketing "chat with your book" alongside PDF-to-audio, study guides, quizzes, and concept maps [16]. Representative of a long tail of small "chat with your book" apps (a Reddit thread titled "Not another chat-with-your-book app" was surfaced in search, suggesting category crowding at the low end — anecdotal signal only).
- **Generic LLM chat (ChatGPT/Claude/etc. over a book PDF)** — **[F]** this is what Farsi-speaking users effectively do today; an Iranian tech blog's roundup of AI book-summarization tools points to international services and to uploading the book PDF to ChatGPT-like assistants as the practical route, with Iranian AI text tools like Summarify offering Persian summarization but not dialogue over a specific book [27].

**What this category delivers**: conversational access to (some of) the book's content, sometimes with citations (NotebookLM), sometimes spoiler-aware (Kindle), usually without any pedagogy, verification surface, or session-long research state.

## 4. Courses and communities

- **[F]** Book clubs and reading communities — Goodreads (Amazon-owned) runs the annual Reading Challenge; The StoryGraph is the fast-growing independent tracker alternative; both center on tracking and social motivation, not on learning a given book's content [26]. Shortform bundles "community discussions" into its paid product [3].
- **[F]** Cohort-style book learning exists mostly as instructor-led courses around a book (author workshops, Maven-style cohort courses, MOOC units) — none of the sources gathered here describe a scalable, citation-honest, per-book guided format; this corner stayed thin in this research pass and deserves a second look before Phase 0 closes.
- **Farsi market — see §5**: Ketabrah sells ebooks/audiobooks and cross-device reading [21]; no verified Farsi "guided course over one book" product was found in this pass.

**What this category delivers**: schedule, accountability, and social pressure — the classic answers to the completion problem [12] — but verification of learning is left to discussion, not to any system.

## 5. Farsi-speaking market notes

Verified products (Iranian/Persian-first):

- **Fidibo (فیدیبو)** — **[F]** self-described first and most complete Persian app for audiobooks, ebooks, educational content and podcasts, 100,000+ titles, iOS/Android, offline listening, student discounts [17]. A store/reader, not a learning tool.
- **Taaghche (طاقچه)** — **[F]** "Taaghche Biz-nahayat" (unlimited) subscription: thousands of ebooks, audiobooks, magazines; per its official audiobook page the subscription covers 29,000+ volumes out of a 105,000-title catalog; weekly/monthly/6-month/annual plans [18].
- **Ketabrah (کتابراه)** — **[F]** ebook/audiobook storefront across ~20 categories, apps for phone/tablet/PC, publisher portal, promotional merchandising; homepage invites users to "join millions of users" [21].
- **Navaar (نوار)** — **[F]** subscription audio platform whose "**کپسول**" (Capsule) product is exactly a Farsi Blinkist: "خلاصه کتاب‌های صوتی غیر داستانی... در زمان ۱۵ تا ۳۰ دقیقه" (nonfiction audio book summaries in 15–30 minutes, straight to your questions, no fluff), one subscription for unlimited access; capsules include translated bestsellers (e.g., *21 Lessons for the 21st Century*, *Deep Work*, *Atomic Habits*) [19].
- **Bookapo (بوکاپو)** — **[F]** Iranian summary app: "چکیده و نکات کلیدی برترین کتاب‌های شناخته شده در جهان را در ۳۰ دقیقه" — text and audio digests of world bestsellers in ~30 minutes, hundreds of summaries across 19 categories, offline use, business/self-growth skew [20].
- **Audio summary podcasts / micro-books** — **[F]** BPLUS (بی‌پلاس) Persian book-summary podcast [22]; Goushbede (گوش بده) audio summaries of books and podcasts [22]; Microketab (میکروکتاب, by Sabketo) micro-books by subscription — monthly unlimited plan listed at 28,000 Toman at access date [22].
- **AI over Farsi books** — **[F]** no Farsi-first product offering citation-honest dialogue over a specific book's own text was found in this pass; Iranian AI offerings surfaced are summarizers (e.g., Summarify), and the practical recommendation in Farsi tech media is uploading book PDFs to ChatGPT-class assistants [27]. **Absence of evidence within this search budget is not evidence of absence** — a dedicated Persian-language competitive sweep (Cafe Bazaar, Divar/Telavand ecosystems, Telegram channels) remains open work.

Market context specific to the current Book set:

- **[F]** The deployed prototype's Book set is two works of Islamic thought. *طرح کلی اندیشهٔ اسلامی در قرآن* is described by Persian Wikipedia as the author's fifth written work, printed in 1354 (1975), from tafsir sessions in Mashhad [23]. *انسان ۲۵۰ ساله* is published by Sahba (مؤسسهٔ فرهنگی هنری ایمان جهادی), subtitled as selections of Ali Khamenei's speeches on the political lives of the Shia Imams, issued in multiple "حلقه" print runs, available in print and as an ebook, including a free summary of "حلقه دوم" on Taaghche [24]. Both books sit in the religious-thought segment of the Farsi market, not the business/self-help segment that Farsi summary apps (Bookapo, Navaar capsules) currently serve [19][20].
- **[H — unsourced, flagged honestly]** Religious and ideological books in Iran are widely studied in group study circles (حلقه‌های مطالعه) and mosque/community sessions; a guided, page-cited digital companion maps naturally onto that culture. This research found no citable source for the prevalence of such circles — treat as PM/domain knowledge to confirm, not a sourced fact.

Honest gaps in the Farsi picture (not resolvable within this ticket's search budget):

- No sourced data on Farsi e-book/audiobook market size, piracy rates, or Willingness-to-pay benchmarks was found — none is asserted here.
- No Farsi-language pricing for Navaar/Bookapo/Microketab beyond Microketab's listed Toman price [22] was captured; subscription prices for Navaar/Bookapo remain unverified.
- Whether global tools (NotebookLM, Kindle "Ask This Book") work acceptably in Farsi is untested here — NotebookLM's docs don't list Persian among voice languages [8]; Kindle's feature is English-books-only today [14].

## 6. Facts vs hypotheses ledger

**Facts (sourced)**

1. Rereading/highlighting/summarizing = low-utility; retrieval practice + spacing = the only high-utility techniques in Dunlosky et al. 2013 [10]. Retrieval beats restudy for retention, and learners mispredict this (Karpicke & Roediger 2008) [11].
2. Book non-completion is a famous consumer pattern; the Hawking Index is the best-known (unscientific) proxy [12].
3. Summary apps sell 15–30 min digests; verified prices: Blinkist $99.99/yr (list) with PRO $174.99/yr [1], €15.99/€21.99 monthly tiers [2]; Shortform $24/mo or $16.42/mo annual with exercises + community [3]; Headway $19.99/4wk, $39.99/12wk, $59.99/24wk [5]; Instaread prices not displayed on fetched pages [4]. None claim page-cited answers to the book itself.
4. Go1 (enterprise learning) acquired Blinkist in 2023 [15].
5. NotebookLM/Gemini Notebook is source-grounded with inline citations, many-source, multi-format outputs, free + paid tiers, now also bundled into Workspace; no chat history [8][9].
6. Amazon ships AI over its books: Recaps (Apr 2025) [13], "Ask This Book" (US, iOS, English, spoiler-aware, no author opt-out, rights backlash) [14].
7. Basmo ChatBook markets chat-with-your-book without disclosing quote/citation mechanics [7].
8. Farsi market has stores/readers (Fidibo [17], Taaghche subscription [18], Ketabrah [21]) and summary products (Navaar capsules 15–30 min [19], Bookapo 30-min digests [20], BPLUS/Goushbede/Microketab [22]); no Farsi citation-honest chat-over-book product was found [27].
9. The prototype's two books are works of Islamic thought, one with 1975 origins in Mashhad tafsir sessions [23], the other a Sahba-published multi-print-run title on the political lives of the Imams [24].

**Hypotheses (to be tested before go/no-go)**

- [H1] Summary apps and audiobook subscriptions serve the "feel informed cheaply" job, not the "actually learn this book" job; the two jobs have different buyers.
- [H2] The only AI neighbor with genuine citation honesty (NotebookLM) is a horizontal research assistant drifting enterprise-ward (Workspace bundling [8], Go1-style consolidation elsewhere [15]) and English-first — it will not serve a Farsi consumer learning one religious-thought book well, soon.
- [H3] Kindle "Ask This Book" is the shape of the platform threat: if Amazon extends it beyond English/iOS, closed-ecosystem chat-over-book becomes a default for English books — but it stays Q&A chrome on a storefront, not a guided learning journey with verifiable outcomes [14].
- [H4] The learning-science wedge is real and unclaimed: no competitor found implements retrieval practice, spacing, or guided questioning *over the book's own text* — the only techniques rated high-utility [10].
- [H5] Farsi-first is a wedge, not a moat: it has no citation-honest competitor today (within this search budget) [27], but localization is imitable; the defensible core is citation honesty + pedagogy, with Farsi as the entry market.
- [H6 — unsourced]** Study-circle culture in Iran makes guided book-completion socially legible; needs a real source or PM confirmation before use in the Phase 0 proposal.

## 7. DRAFT differentiation hypothesis (marked as hypothesis)

**Draft hypothesis (for grilling, not a conclusion):**

> For the Farsi-speaking reader who wants to *actually learn one book* — starting with works of Islamic thought like the prototype's Book set — every existing option fails the same test: it either replaces the book with a digest (Blinkist, Shortform, Navaar capsules, Bookapo [1][3][19][20]), chats without verifiable grounding (Basmo ChatBook [7], ChatGPT-over-PDF [27]), grounds and cites but neither teaches nor guides nor speaks Farsi (NotebookLM [8][9]), or is locked inside a closed English-only ecosystem (Kindle "Ask This Book" [14]). The rebranded product's defensible claim is the *combination* no sourced competitor offers:
>
> 1. **Citation honesty over the book's own text** — every claim page-cited to the very book being learned, verbatim quotes, no invented pages, gaps declared as gaps (CONTEXT.md's Citation/Gap vocabulary);
> 2. **Guided agentic pedagogy** — an agent that asks guided questions and makes the learner retrieve and connect, exploiting the only high-utility techniques in the evidence base [10][11], instead of dumping an answer;
> 3. **One-book depth, not library breadth** — the product is "learn *this* book," the opposite of every summary app's library pitch;
> 4. **Farsi-first delivery** in a market whose learning products are stores and summaries [17]–[22] with no citation-honest AI alternative found [27].
>
> The one-line positioning candidate: *«کتاب را با عاملی که چیزی از خودش نمی‌گوید بیاموز» — learn the book with an agent that never speaks for itself; every sentence it claims, the book itself says on a page you can open.*

**Falsifiers / risks the PM should grill (all hypotheses, per §6):**

- NotebookLM or a local giant adds Farsi + a guided mode (platform risk, H2).
- The actual B2C job in this segment is "feel informed" — in which case summary apps already won and a deep-learning product has a small addressable audience (H1, supported weakly by the completion pattern [12]).
- Kindle-style Q&A is "good enough" and free-adjacent where the books are bought (H3).
- Study-circle culture claim is unsourced (H6) and the Islamic-thought segment may prefer human teachers over agents.
- Farsi-first is a wedge, not a moat (H5).
